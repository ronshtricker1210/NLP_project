\begin{abstract}
Reasoning models perform strongly on clean mathematical and scientific
benchmarks, but real users often write with typos. We study how these everyday
errors affect not only final-answer accuracy, but also the reasoning process
itself.

We introduce a controlled typo generator that independently varies the number of
corrupted words and the proportion of typos that produce another valid English
word. We apply it to 500 questions from each of GSM8K, MATH-500, and
ARC-Challenge, and evaluate DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B across all corruption
settings.

Typos consistently reduce accuracy, with the largest effect on GSM8K, where
performance falls from 91.4\% on clean questions to 60.4\% in the most corrupted
setting. More importantly, typos that produce another valid English word are
consistently more harmful than non-word typos. Depending on the dataset, a
real-word typo is estimated to be 1.9 to 5.1 times more damaging per typo.

The reasoning traces show that the model often notices that something is wrong:
corrupted inputs lead to longer reasoning and more uncertainty language.
However, this does not translate into more effective self-correction. The model
hesitates more, but does not verify more.

Finally, none of the lightweight mitigation strategies we tested consistently
improved performance. Warning the model does not improve performance, asking it
to rewrite the question first can make performance worse, and spell checking
becomes less effective as the proportion of real-word typos increases. Overall,
our results suggest that the main challenge is not only detecting that the input
is corrupted, but correctly recovering the intended meaning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% AUTHOR TEXT - verbatim.
% =====================================================================

Chain-of-thought prompting and reasoning-focused models have substantially
improved performance on mathematical and scientific benchmarks
\citep{wei2022,deepseekr1}. However, benchmark questions are usually carefully
written, while real user inputs often contain typos such as swapped letters,
missing characters, or nearby-key mistakes.

This gap motivates our study: real users often make simple typing mistakes when
interacting with LLMs, so strong performance on clean benchmark text may
overestimate reliability in real-world use.

Previous work has shown that typos can hurt LLM performance, often linking this
effect to subword tokenization \citep{gan2024,chai2024,zhao2026}. However, these
studies mainly focus on final-answer accuracy and do not examine what happens
inside the reasoning process.

Reasoning models make this question especially interesting. Their long reasoning
traces give them many opportunities to notice a corrupted word, infer what was
intended, and recover before producing the final answer. Do they actually do so?
We study this question using a controlled two-axis experimental design.

To study this, our typo generator separates two factors: how many words are
corrupted and what kind of corruption they contain. In particular, we vary the
share of typos that accidentally produce another valid English word. This allows
us to compare real-word and non-word typos while keeping the total number of
typos fixed and using the same questions in every condition - a comparison that
previous work has not made.

We evaluate DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B \citep{deepseekr1} across the full set of
conditions on three datasets with different reasoning characteristics. Beyond
measuring final-answer accuracy, we also examine the reasoning traces themselves
using word-level alignment and an LLM judge.

Our main contributions are: (i) a controlled experimental setup that
independently varies typo rate and the proportion of real-word typos across
three datasets, allowing us to directly compare real-word and non-word
corruptions while keeping the overall typo rate fixed. (ii) an analysis of how
typos affect the reasoning process itself, including changes in model
uncertainty, whether the model notices corrupted words, and whether it attempts
to repair them, and (iii) an evaluation of three simple mitigation strategies:
warning the model that the input may contain typos, asking it to correct
possible typos before solving the problem, and applying an external spellchecker
before sending the prompt to the model.

\section{Background and Related Work}
% AUTHOR TEXT - verbatim.
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Sensitivity to character-level typos.}
\citet{belinkov2018} show that both synthetic and natural character-level noise
substantially degrade neural machine translation, and that robustness to one
type of noise does not necessarily transfer to others. Later work studied
similar vulnerabilities in adversarial settings. HotFlip \citep{ebrahimi2018}
shows that a few white-box character changes can alter a classifier's
prediction, while TextFooler \citep{jin2020} generates black-box adversarial
examples through word substitutions. PromptRobust \citep{zhu2024} shows that
small changes to task prompts can substantially reduce LLM performance,
including changes at both the character and word levels. \citet{pruthi2019}
defend against adversarial misspellings by placing a robust word-recognition
model before the classifier. In one experiment, an adversarial character attack
reduced BERT accuracy from 90.3\% to 45.8\%, while their defense recovered it to
75\%. This approach is closely related to the spell-checking mitigation we
evaluate.

\paragraph{Typos and modern LLMs.}
Recent studies show that typos can substantially reduce LLM performance.
\citet{gan2024} use an optimized adversarial search to inject typos into
reasoning benchmarks such as GSM8K, BBH, and MMLU, and show that model accuracy
drops substantially as the number of character-level errors increases.
\citet{chai2024} show that character-level typos can hurt model performance
partly because they change how words are split into subwords.
\citet{zhao2026} study typo robustness across several languages and 18
open-source models. They find that typos hurt performance most on tasks that
require generation or more complex reasoning.

These studies measure final-answer accuracy rather than the reasoning process
itself, and they vary typos by generation mechanism rather than by whether the
corrupted form is itself a valid word. That distinction matters. A typo that
creates a non-word, such as \texttt{triangle\arrow trianlge}, mainly affects
tokenization, while a typo that creates another valid word, such as
\texttt{sum\arrow sun}, leaves tokenization intact and changes the meaning of
the sentence. Our study separates these cases and examines how the model
responds to each during reasoning.

\paragraph{Reasoning traces and self-correction.}
Reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 \citep{deepseekr1} produce long reasoning
traces before giving a final answer, allowing us to examine how they respond to
errors during reasoning. However, these traces do not always fully reflect the
model's internal reasoning \citep{turpin2023}, and prior work shows that LLMs
often struggle to correct their own reasoning without external feedback,
sometimes even degrading their answers in the process \citep{huang2024}.

Our setting asks a related but different question: can a long reasoning process
help the model deal with errors that are already present in the input? A typo
may be detectable from context, giving the model an opportunity to notice it,
infer the intended word, and continue reasoning correctly. This allows us to
study whether additional reasoning actually helps models recover from noisy
input.

\section{Method}
% AUTHOR TEXT - verbatim.
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Controlled typo generation}

We generate corrupted versions of each question by modifying only its
natural-language text. Numbers, \LaTeX{} expressions, and \LaTeX{} command
names are protected and never modified. Candidate words must consist only of
ASCII letters, and we exclude single-character words and a small set of common
function words.

The generator varies two factors independently. The typo rate $r$ determines
how many eligible words are corrupted, while the real-word ratio $\rho$
determines the target fraction of those corruptions that should result in
another valid English word. For example, \texttt{eats}~\arrow~\texttt{east}
is a real-word typo, while \texttt{ducks}~\arrow~\texttt{dcks} is a non-word
typo. Words are selected for corruption before $\rho$ is applied, so for a
fixed typo rate the number of corrupted words is identical across all values
of $\rho$.

Each corrupted word receives a character-level edit sampled with weights 0.40
adjacent-key substitution, 0.20 deletion, 0.20 insertion, and 0.20
transposition. With probability 0.10, a second edit is applied to the same
word.

For each question, we convert $\rho$ into a target number of real-word typos,
using probabilistic rounding when the target is not an integer. We then process
the selected words in random order. While the target has not been reached, the
generator searches for valid real-word variants using the NLTK English lexicon.
If none exists for a selected word, that word receives a non-word typo and the
generator continues trying to satisfy the target with the remaining words. Any
real word produced accidentally is counted toward the target. Because the
selected words are not chosen based on whether a real-word variant exists, the
requested ratio cannot always be reached exactly. In these cases, the generator
gets as close to the target as the selected words allow.
Table~\ref{tab:datastats} reports the achieved ratios.

For each benchmark, we create a clean condition that is identical to the
original dataset, together with nine corrupted conditions. These combine typo
rates $r \in \{25, 50, 75\}\%$ with real-word ratios
$\rho \in \{10, 40, 70\}\%$. The generator is seeded consistently across
conditions, and the same questions are used in every condition, allowing direct
paired comparisons. The resulting dataset variants are available on the Hugging
Face Hub.

\subsection{Datasets}

We use 500 questions from each of three benchmarks: GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021},
MATH-500 \citep{hendrycks2021,lightman2024}, and ARC-Challenge
\citep{clark2018}. We chose these datasets because they differ in how much of
the information needed to solve a question is expressed in natural-language
text.

GSM8K contains arithmetic word problems in which most of the problem structure
is described in prose. Its questions are also relatively long, so more words
are available for corruption. MATH-500 contains competition mathematics
problems that are shorter and contain substantial \LaTeX{} content, which is
protected by our generator. ARC-Challenge contains shorter multiple-choice
science questions, where the answer options provide additional information that
may help the model interpret a corrupted question.

These differences mean that the same typo rate does not produce the same
absolute number of typos across datasets. GSM8K receives the most corruption
per question, while MATH-500 and ARC-Challenge receive fewer corruptions
because they contain fewer eligible words. We therefore compare corruption
levels within each dataset rather than treating a given typo rate as the same
absolute amount of noise across all three benchmarks.
Table~\ref{tab:datastats} reports the resulting corruption statistics.

\section{Experimental Setup}
% AUTHOR TEXT - verbatim.
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Models.}
We use DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B \citep{deepseekr1} as our main model and
access it through the Hugging Face API. We run it with temperature 0.6 and
top-$p$ 0.95, using the default settings from the model card, and generate one
response per question. We use the recommended decoding settings to better
reflect how a typical user would interact with the model, rather than tuning
them specifically for our evaluation. For each dataset, we set the maximum
generation length to a value that we found sufficient for the model to produce
complete answers for a large enough portion of the questions.\footnote{In
practice this is 20{,}000 generated tokens for GSM8K and 17{,}000 for MATH-500
and ARC-Challenge.}

For the LLM-based evaluation, we use Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct \citep{llama3}, also
through the Hugging Face API, with temperature 0.

\paragraph{Prompts.}
For math problems, we provide the question followed by the instruction:
``Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}.'' For multiple-choice questions, we present
the four answer options with shuffled labels and ask the model to return the
final answer as a boxed letter. Typos are added only to the question itself,
while the instruction remains unchanged and typo-free.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
We extract the final answer only from the text produced after the closing
\texttt{</think>} tag. Responses that reach the token limit without producing a
final answer are marked as unanswered. GSM8K is evaluated using normalized
numeric matching, MATH-500 using symbolic equivalence with
\texttt{math\_verify}, and ARC using exact answer-letter matching. We report
accuracy over answered questions and separately report the number of answered
responses.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Since each condition contains the same 500 questions, we use paired statistical
tests. We report the number of questions that change from correct to incorrect
and from incorrect to correct, and use a continuity-corrected McNemar test to
compare these paired outcomes. This analysis helps separate the effect of typos
from differences in question difficulty, since each comparison is made on the
same set of questions.

\paragraph{Reasoning trace analysis.}
We analyze the model's reasoning traces in three ways. First, we use word-level
alignment to track how the model handles each corrupted word. We check whether
the trace uses the original word, the corrupted version, or an explicit
correction, and classify each typo as silently fixed, flagged, misread, or not
used.

Second, we measure self-doubt directly from the reasoning traces. We define sets
of words and phrases associated with uncertainty and self-correction, and count
how often they appear in each trace. Since traces differ in length, we normalize
these counts by trace length. We then compare these measures across typo
conditions and between correct and incorrect answers to examine whether higher
uncertainty is associated with model failure.

Third, we use an LLM judge. The judge receives the clean
question, the corrupted question, the list of changed words, and the model's
reasoning trace. Using a predefined evaluation prompt, it assigns a self-doubt
score from 0 to 10, which measures the ``amount of unambiguity'' and a repair
behavior score from 0 to 5 which measures how much the model understands the
real meaning of the question.

\section{Results}
% AUTHOR TEXT - verbatim.
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Effect of typos on accuracy}

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy} and Table~\ref{tab:grid} in the appendix show the full
results. On GSM8K, answered-only accuracy drops from 91.4\% on clean questions
to 60.4\% in the most corrupted condition ($r{=}75$, $\rho{=}70$), a decrease of
31.0 percent. ARC shows a smaller drop from 84.7\% to 74.7\%, while
MATH-500 drops from 95.2\% to 86.9\%. Overall, accuracy decreases as the typo
rate increases across all three datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy}).

The paired analysis shows the same trend. In the most corrupted GSM8K condition,
164 questions change from correct to incorrect, while only 14 change from
incorrect to correct, out of 480 questions answered in both conditions
($p < 10^{-6}$). ARC shows 75 correct-to-incorrect versus 26
incorrect-to-correct changes, and MATH-500 shows 46 versus 7, both statistically
significant. GSM8K is affected even at the mildest typo condition, while
significant effects appear only at higher typo rates for MATH-500 and ARC
(Table~\ref{tab:grid}).

One possible explanation for these differences is how much each task depends on
the wording of the question. GSM8K problems rely heavily on the text to describe
the quantities and relationships needed for the solution. ARC provides
multiple-choice options that may help the model recover some information, while
much of the important mathematical content in MATH-500 is written in protected
\LaTeX{} and is therefore not affected by our typo generator.

Typos also lead to longer reasoning traces across all three datasets, while
the model still produces final answers in most cases. On GSM8K, for example,
the average reasoning length increases from 1,582 tokens on clean questions to
2,594 tokens at $r{=}75$, $\rho{=}40$, with smaller increases on MATH-500 and
ARC. This suggests that the drop in accuracy is driven mainly by incorrect
answers rather than incomplete generations. In fact, wrong answers tend to have
longer reasoning traces than correct ones, indicating that additional reasoning
is more closely associated with difficulty than with successful recovery
(Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy}c; Table~\ref{tab:support}).

\subsection{Real-word typos are more harmful}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_realword.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The real-word effect.} \emph{(a)} multiplicative loss in the
odds of a correct answer per additional typo, from a logistic regression fitting
real-word and non-word counts separately. \emph{(b)} the controlled paired
comparison: the same questions with the same number of typos, changing only the
real-word share from $\rho{=}10$ to $\rho{=}70$. $^{*}$ marks McNemar
$p<0.05$.}
\label{fig:realword}
\end{figure}


Because typo rate and real-word ratio are varied independently, we can compare
real-word and non-word typos while controlling for the total number of typos.
For each dataset, we fit a logistic regression using the number of real-word and
non-word typos to predict whether the final answer is correct
(Figure~\ref{fig:realword}a).

The results show that an additional real-word typo has a stronger negative
association with correctness than an additional non-word typo. Based on the
estimated odds multipliers from the logistic regression, the relative per-typo
loss is about 1.9$\times$ larger on GSM8K, 1.3$\times$ on MATH-500, and
5.3$\times$ on ARC (Table~\ref{tab:realword-logit}).

The paired comparisons support this pattern, although the effect depends on the
overall typo rate. On GSM8K at a 75\% typo rate, increasing the real-word share
from 10\% to 70\% lowers accuracy by 14.1 percentage points, even though the
same questions contain the same total number of typos. The difference is also
significant at a 50\% typo rate, but not at 25\%. This suggests that when
corruption is relatively mild, the model can often handle both real-word and
non-word typos, while the difference between the two becomes more pronounced as
the overall typo rate increases (Figure~\ref{fig:realword}b;
Table~\ref{tab:realword-paired}).

ARC shows a significant real-word effect at the highest typo rate, while
MATH-500 does not show a significant difference at any typo rate. This is
consistent with the smaller number of corrupted words in MATH-500 questions,
since much of their mathematical content is protected from modification. All
nine contrasts, for all three datasets, are in
Table~\ref{tab:realword-paired}.

Overall, the results suggest that real-word typos can be especially difficult
for the model. Unlike non-word typos, which often produce visibly unusual word
forms, real-word typos result in valid words and can therefore change the
meaning of the input without creating an obvious tokenization problem.


\subsection{Repair behaviour: noticed but not repaired}

\begin{figure*}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_repair.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Word-level repair behaviour.} How every corrupted word is
handled in the reasoning trace. \emph{(a)} against the typo rate, pooled over
real-word ratios. \emph{(b)} against the real-word ratio, pooled over typo
rates. Section~5.3 claims a trend on both axes and both are present: silent
fixing gives way to explicit flagging and to outright misreading as either
knob is turned up, while the share never referred to at all stays roughly
constant. Per-configuration values are in Table~\ref{tab:repair}.}
\label{fig:repair}
\end{figure*}


We recover each corrupted word by comparing the clean and corrupted versions of
the question, and then classify how the model handles it in the reasoning trace:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{silent fix}: only the original word appears, suggesting that the
  model recovered the intended word without explicitly mentioning the typo
  \item \textbf{flagged}: both the original and corrupted forms appear,
  indicating that the model noticed the typo and corrected it
  \item \textbf{misread}: only the corrupted form appears, suggesting that the
  model interpreted it as the wrong word
  \item \textbf{not used}: neither form appears in the reasoning trace
\end{itemize}

We originally expected real-word typos to go unnoticed more often, while
non-word typos would be easier to detect and correct explicitly. The results
only partly support this prediction.

Both a higher typo rate and a higher real-word ratio change the way the model
handles corrupted words. As the typo rate increases from 25\% to 75\%, silent
fixing decreases across all three datasets. On GSM8K, for example, it falls from
73.3\% to 55.8\% at $\rho{=}10$. Similar decreases appear on ARC and MATH-500.
At the same time, flagging and misreading become more common, and misreading
roughly doubles on all three datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:repair};
Table~\ref{tab:repair}).

Increasing the real-word ratio produces a similar effect. Pooled across typo
rates on GSM8K, silent fixing falls from 62.3\% to 52.9\% as $\rho$ increases
from 10 to 70, while flagging rises from 21.0\% to 26.8\% and misreading from
5.4\% to 7.7\%. The same overall pattern appears on MATH-500 and ARC. Misreading
is also closely related to failure: it occurs 1.2 to 2.0 times more often in
incorrect answers than in correct ones (Table~\ref{tab:repair}).

The proportion of typos classified as not used remains relatively stable within
each dataset, but differs across tasks: roughly 12\% on GSM8K, 13\% on ARC, and
19\% on MATH-500. One possible explanation is that MATH-500 reasoning relies
more heavily on symbolic manipulation, so the model refers back to individual
words from the original question less often (Figure~\ref{fig:repair}).

In the LLM-as-a-judge analysis, a separate language model examined each
reasoning trace and assigned a score for repair behaviour. The judge was shown
the corrupted words, the question, and the model's reasoning trace. It then gave
a score from 0 to 5: 0 means that the model fully understood the meaning of the
question, while 5 means that it completely failed to understand the intended
meaning. We then calculated the mean score for different typo rates, real-word
ratios, and final-answer outcomes.

The results show that more typos generally make the question harder to
understand and repair. The average repair-understanding score increases as the
typo rate rises, meaning that the model loses more of the original meaning when
more words are corrupted. Wrong answers also receive higher average
repair-understanding scores than correct answers, supporting the connection
between misunderstanding the corrupted question and failure. The real-word ratio
has an additional effect: real-word typos look valid and can silently
change the meaning of a sentence, making them harder to detect and repair than
obviously invalid non-word typos (Table~\ref{tab:judge}).

\subsection{Self-doubt}


We measure self-doubt using two curated sets of expressions found in the
reasoning traces. The uncertainty set includes hedging expressions such as
\emph{maybe}, \emph{perhaps}, \emph{possibly}, \emph{might be}, and \emph{not
sure}. The second-guessing set captures explicit reconsideration or
self-correction, with expressions such as \emph{wait}, \emph{actually},
\emph{reconsider}, \emph{let me recheck}, and \emph{double-check}. To account
for differences in trace length, we report the number of matches per 1,000
reasoning words.

Uncertainty increases as the input becomes more corrupted. On GSM8K, for
example, uncertainty rises from 7.4 markers per 1,000 words on clean input to
12.3 at a 25\% typo rate and 18.7 at a 75\% typo rate, when the real-word ratio
is held at 10\%. The same increasing pattern appears on ARC and MATH-500.
Increasing the real-word ratio at a fixed typo rate leads to an additional rise
in uncertainty (Figure~\ref{fig:doubt}; Table~\ref{tab:support}).

Second-guessing shows a different pattern. Its density increases only slightly
between clean and corrupted inputs and then remains relatively stable across
typo rates and real-word ratios. Although the total number of second-guessing
expressions per trace increases, this is largely explained by the fact that
corrupted inputs produce longer reasoning traces. In other words, typos make the
model express more uncertainty, but do not appear to make it reconsider its
reasoning more often (Figure~\ref{fig:doubt}).

The difference becomes clearer when we separate correct and incorrect answers.
For the same typo rate and real-word ratio, uncertainty expressions are
consistently more frequent in traces that end with a wrong answer. On GSM8K,
uncertainty density is about 1.4 to 2.1 times higher in incorrect traces, with
similar patterns on ARC and MATH-500. Second-guessing shows a much weaker
difference between correct and incorrect answers (Table~\ref{tab:support}).

Overall, uncertainty language is strongly associated with model failure, while
explicit self-correction is not. The model expresses more doubt as the input
becomes noisier and also tends to express more doubt when it is about to give a
wrong answer. However, this relationship is not unique to typo-corrupted inputs:
even on clean GSM8K questions, incorrect answers already contain substantially
more uncertainty language than correct ones.

In the LLM-as-a-judge analysis, a separate language model examined each
reasoning trace and assigned a self-doubt score from 0 to 10. A score of 0 means
that the model followed a clear, straight-line solution without hesitation,
while 10 means that the trace contained pervasive uncertainty, repeated
second-guessing, reversals, or looping. The judge was shown the corrupted words,
the question, and the model's reasoning trace (like in 5.3).

On all three datasets, the average self-doubt score increases as the typo rate
rises, indicating that more corrupted words cause more hesitation and
re-evaluation. Typo traces also score higher than clean traces everywhere. The
real-word ratio, by contrast, has almost no effect: at a fixed typo rate the
score moves by at most about 0.1 point in either direction. The amount of
corruption changes how much the model hesitates, while the kind of corruption
does not (Table~\ref{tab:judge}).

\subsection{Every lightweight mitigation fails}

We tested the three mitigation strategies proposed in our plan, all on GSM8K (Figure~\ref{fig:fixes}, Table~\ref{tab:fixes}).

\paragraph{Warn.}
We first added a short warning that the input may contain typos. Across the nine
typo configurations, this changed accuracy by only $-0.5$ percentage points on
average, with effects ranging from $-4.3$ to $+3.5$ points. In other words,
simply warning the model does not improve performance. This is consistent with
the analysis in Section~5.3, where the model often already showed signs of
noticing that the input was corrupted. The main limitation therefore does not
appear to be awareness of the typo itself.

\paragraph{Rewrite.}
We next instructed the model to first rewrite the question with the typos
corrected and only then solve it. This performed worse than the original setup
in all nine configurations, reducing accuracy by 4.7 percentage points on
average and by as much as 7.5 points. Median reasoning length also dropped from
1,214 to about 730 tokens. One possible explanation is that the rewrite step
forces the model to commit to a single interpretation of the corrupted question
too early. If the reconstruction is wrong, the model then reasons about the
wrong version of the problem with little opportunity to recover. The additional
rewrite step may also shift part of the generation budget away from the
reasoning process itself.

\paragraph{Spell check.}
We applied an external spellchecker to the same 500 GSM8K questions before
sending them to the model. A typo was considered recovered only if it was
restored exactly to the original word. Recovery dropped as the share of
real-word typos increased: from 54.9\% at $\rho{=}10$ to 39.9\% at
$\rho{=}40$ and 25.8\% at $\rho{=}70$. However, this lexical recovery did not
translate into a consistent accuracy gain. Across the nine typo
configurations, the spellchecker changed accuracy by only $-0.1$ percentage
points on average, improving five conditions and hurting four; accuracy in the
hardest condition was 62.2\% (Table~\ref{tab:fixes}).

This is likely because spellcheckers are much better at detecting non-word
errors than real-word substitutions, which already look like valid words. In
some cases, they may even replace a non-word typo with the wrong valid word,
effectively creating the type of error that Section~5.2 found to be more
harmful.

Taken together, these results suggest that the main difficulty is not simply
detecting that the input contains errors. None of the three strategies reliably
recovers the information that was lost during corruption: the identity of the
original word. Warnings provide little additional information, an explicit
rewrite step can commit the model to an incorrect reconstruction, and
dictionary-based correction is least effective precisely when the corruption
results in another valid word.

\section{Discussion}
% AUTHOR TEXT - verbatim.
% =====================================================================

The most consistent finding across our analyses is that real-word typos are more
harmful than non-word typos. This pattern appears in the per-typo regression,
the paired comparisons, and the spellchecker results. At first, this may seem
surprising, since non-word typos often produce unusual tokenization patterns and
might therefore be expected to be more disruptive. Our results suggest the
opposite. A non-word typo is visibly unusual and can serve as a signal that
something is wrong, while a real-word typo is fluent, tokenized normally, and
may fit the sentence well enough to go unnoticed even when its meaning is
incorrect.

This also helps explain the limited success of dictionary-based correction. Such
methods are effective mainly when the corrupted form is not a valid word.
Real-word substitutions, however, are already present in the dictionary and are
therefore much harder to detect. In our spellchecker experiment, recovery
dropped substantially as the proportion of real-word typos increased. In some
cases, the spellchecker also replaced a non-word typo with an incorrect valid
word, effectively turning a visible error into the type of corruption we found
to be more damaging.

The trace analysis points to a related conclusion. The model often appears to
notice that the input is unusual: uncertainty language increases, suspicious
words are flagged more often, and reasoning traces become longer as corruption
increases. However, this additional effort does not reliably lead to recovery.
The model expresses more uncertainty, but does not appear to verify its
interpretation more effectively.

This helps explain why the simple mitigation strategies we tested had little
effect. Warning the model adds information it often already has, while forcing
it to rewrite the question can make it commit too early to a single
interpretation. If that reconstruction is wrong, the subsequent reasoning is
based on the wrong version of the problem.

Taken together, these results suggest that the main difficulty is not detecting
that a typo exists, but recovering the intended word when the corruption is
ambiguous. A more promising direction may therefore be to preserve multiple
possible interpretations during reasoning and resolve the ambiguity later using
the structure of the problem, rather than forcing an early correction.

Finally, longer reasoning traces should not be interpreted as evidence of
successful self-correction. In our results, incorrect answers tend to produce
longer traces even on clean input. This suggests that additional reasoning often
reflects difficulty rather than successful recovery.

\section{Limitations}
% AUTHOR TEXT - verbatim.
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{One model, one sample.}
All headline results come from a single 7B reasoning model at one decoding
setting with $n{=}1$ sample per question. Our plan called for several samples per
question, but the API budget did not permit it, so we cannot separate typo
effects from decoding variance at the level of an individual question. Still,
the overall effects are much larger than expected sampling noise (McNemar
$p < 10^{-6}$ on $n{=}480$ paired items), but generalisation to larger reasoning
models is untested.

\paragraph{Dataset substitution.}
We originally planned to use GPQA-Diamond, but replaced it with ARC-Challenge,
which is also a multiple-choice science dataset. On clean GPQA-Diamond, the
model reached only about 52\% accuracy. Since random guessing gives 25\%, this
leaves limited room to measure further drops under corruption: as performance
gets closer to chance, changes in accuracy become harder to interpret as
reasoning failures rather than guessing. ARC-Challenge is easier and its answer
options may help the model recover some information, so our multiple-choice
results may underestimate the effect of typos on harder science questions.

\paragraph{Mitigations and human validation.}
We tested the three mitigation methods only on GSM8K because the experiments
required additional paid API calls and our budget was limited. We chose to focus
on GSM8K because it showed the clearest drop in answer quality under typo
corruption, making it the most informative dataset for evaluating whether these
mitigation strategies could recover performance.

\section{Conclusion}
% AUTHOR TEXT - verbatim.
% =====================================================================

Typos have a clear and consistent effect on reasoning performance. On GSM8K,
accuracy dropped by as much as 31 percentage points, with larger declines as the
corruption rate increased. The strongest effect came from typos that turned one
valid English word into another.

Our analysis suggests that the main problem is not that the model fails to
notice corrupted input. In many cases, it appears to recognize that something is
wrong, but it still cannot reliably recover the intended meaning. The reasoning
traces show more uncertainty than actual verification, and the mitigation
methods we tested, including prompting strategies and dictionary-based
correction, did not consistently improve performance.

Overall, these results suggest that robustness to everyday typing errors should
be treated as a separate capability that needs to be developed explicitly,
rather than something that can be assumed from strong benchmark performance.

All code is available in the project repository, and the raw generation logs are
hosted on the Hugging Face Hub (\texttt{ronshtricker/typo-reasoning-results}).
All reported results can be reproduced by running \texttt{download\_data.py}
followed by \texttt{run\_all.py}.
